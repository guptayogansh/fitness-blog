Zone 2 Is Boring. Do It Anyway.

The most common cardio mistake isn't laziness. It's the opposite: every easy run turns into a moderately hard run, because moderately hard feels like training and easy feels like cheating. Do that for a few months and you end up with a large pile of sessions that were too hard to recover from quickly and too easy to drive real adaptation. The middle is where progress goes to die.

What zone 2 actually is

Zone 2 is the top end of the intensity range you can hold while your body clears lactate about as fast as it produces it. Practically: a pace you could hold for an hour or two, breathing rhythmically, having a conversation in full sentences with a slight pause for breath.

What it develops is unglamorous and enormously useful — more capillaries feeding the working muscle, more and better mitochondria inside it, a heart that moves more blood per beat, and a metabolism that leans harder on fat at any given speed. That's the engine. Interval work teaches you to spend the engine; easy work builds it.

Intensity is a stimulus you can only afford twice a week. Volume is a stimulus you can afford almost every day — but only if the volume is genuinely easy.

Three ways to find your pace

1. The talk test (free, and good enough)

Recite a sentence out loud. If you can get through it comfortably but would rather not deliver a monologue, you're about right. If you can chat indefinitely, pick it up slightly. If you're breaking sentences into two-word chunks, you've drifted into zone 3 — which is the default destination for basically everyone.

2. Nose breathing

Run at the fastest pace at which you can breathe entirely through your nose. It's a crude ceiling, but it's nearly impossible to cheat, and it lands most people slightly conservative — which is the correct direction to be wrong in.

3. Heart rate, with caveats

Commonly quoted ranges put zone 2 around 60–70% of maximum heart rate. The problem is the "220 minus age" formula those percentages are usually built on: it carries a standard deviation of roughly ±10–12 beats per minute, which is wide enough to be useless individually. If you're going to use heart rate, anchor it to a measured max or a field test, wear a chest strap rather than a wrist optical sensor, and treat the number as a check on the talk test rather than a replacement for it.

Cardiac drift is normal.

On a long easy session your heart rate will creep up 5–10 bpm over an hour at identical pace — heat, dehydration, and fatigue all push it. Don't slow to a walk chasing a number; hold the effort and let the number go where it goes.

How much, and how to fit it around lifting

Two to four easy sessions of 30–60 minutes a week is a productive dose for someone whose main goal is strength and general health. Endurance athletes go far higher, and roughly 80% of what they do is at this intensity — that ratio is the actual lesson here, not the total volume.

  • Separate hard from hard. Easy cardio on lifting days is fine; put it after the lifting, or several hours later.
  • Pick low-impact modes if your legs are already loaded. Cycling, rowing, incline walking, and the elliptical all build the same engine with a fraction of the eccentric damage a run does.
  • Don't schedule intervals the day before heavy squats. That's one hard day borrowing from another.

The part nobody likes

Done correctly, this training is dull. You will be passed by people who are working less hard than you over a season. Your pace at a given heart rate improves on a timescale of months, not weeks, and the first six weeks feel like nothing is happening.

Then one day the pace that used to be your tempo run is your easy pace, and every hard session you do sits on top of a much larger base. That's the whole trick. Put a podcast on and go slow.
