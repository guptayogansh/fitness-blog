The 5×5 Blueprint: Your First 12 Weeks Under a Barbell

Almost every good beginner program is the same program wearing a different hat: a handful of barbell lifts, performed often, with slightly more weight each session. The magic isn't in the set-and-rep scheme. It's that a novice can adapt to a hard session in about 48 hours, so you can add weight every single time you train — and that opportunity lasts a few months at most. This is how to spend it well.

The program

Two alternating workouts, three non-consecutive days a week — Monday, Wednesday, Friday is fine, so is Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday. You rotate A, B, A one week; B, A, B the next.

Workout ASets × repsWorkout BSets × reps
Back squat5 × 5Back squat5 × 5
Bench press5 × 5Overhead press5 × 5
Barbell row5 × 5Deadlift1 × 5

That's it. Six working sets a day, plus warm-ups. Most sessions take 45–60 minutes, and the majority of that is rest.

Why the deadlift only gets one set

Because it works. A heavy set of five deadlifts is enormously fatiguing relative to how much stimulus the fifth set would add, and the squats you did ten minutes earlier already taxed the same tissue. One hard set, done with intent, progresses for months.

Loading: start embarrassingly light

This is the instruction people ignore, and it's the one that decides whether you're still running the program in week ten. Start at a weight you could do for eight or nine reps. On day one you should leave the gym thinking the whole thing was a waste of time.

Then add, every session:

  • Squat and deadlift: 5 kg / 10 lb per session at first, dropping to 2.5 kg / 5 lb once it stops being easy.
  • Bench, press, row: 2.5 kg / 5 lb per session, dropping to 1 kg / 2 lb with fractional plates when it gets hard.
Buy fractional plates before you need them.

A pair of 0.5 kg / 1 lb plates costs about the same as two protein shakes and will add roughly two months to your overhead press progression. The press adds about 1 kg of capability a week at best; forcing it to jump 2.5 kg is why everyone stalls there first.

Warming up

Five minutes on a bike or rower, then ramp to your working weight on the first exercise of the day. For a 100 kg working squat: bar × 5, 40 × 5, 60 × 3, 80 × 2, then start your work sets. Later exercises need one or two ramp sets at most — you're already warm.

Rest between work sets is not optional and not the place to be efficient. Three minutes when the weight is moderate, five when it's heavy, longer if you need it. If you finish a session in 30 minutes, you rushed, and the last two sets were worse for it.

What "5 reps" actually means

All five sets use the same weight, and all five sets are meant to be completed. This is not a set of five to failure followed by four progressively sadder sets. The first set should feel solidly manageable; the fifth is the one that's a grind. When the fifth set stops being a grind, you added weight too slowly.

A good novice session is boring in the middle and hard at the end. If it's hard at the start, the weight is wrong.

When you miss reps — and you will

Missing is not failure of the program; it's the program telling you the free lunch is over for that lift. Handle it in this order:

  1. Repeat the weight next session. Everyone has bad days. Sleep, eat, come back and hit it.
  2. Miss the same weight a second time? Repeat once more, and be honest about food and sleep in between.
  3. Third miss: deload that lift by 10% and climb back. The re-climb almost always breaks through the old number, because you're recovering while the weight is light.

Deload the lift you missed, not the whole workout. Your squat stalling has nothing to do with your row.

The four ways beginners end their first block early

1. Starting too heavy

Beginning at a weight that already feels hard converts twelve weeks of progression into three. You get to the same number either way, but starting light you arrive with momentum instead of a stall and a sore back.

2. Adding "just a bit" of extra work

Curls are fine. Adding a second squat day, a conditioning circuit, and an arms day is not — recovery is the constraint here, and every extra hard set is drawn from the same account that funds tomorrow's five pounds. Finish the block, then add.

3. Eating like someone who isn't training

Linear progression requires the raw material to build with. If the scale hasn't moved at all in three weeks and your lifts have stopped, you have a nutrition problem wearing a training problem's clothes. See Protein Without a Food Scale.

4. Changing the program

Twelve weeks is not long. The urge to swap exercises around usually shows up in week four, which is precisely when the boring version starts working. Write the twelve dates down and finish them.

What week 12 looks like

A typical untrained adult who starts light, eats enough, and doesn't miss sessions adds something like 40–60 kg to their squat and 20–30 kg to their bench across a first block. Your numbers will differ — starting point, age, sleep, and genetics all get a vote — but the shape of the curve is the same for nearly everybody.

When session-to-session progress finally dies for good on the lower-body lifts, you're not a novice anymore. That's when you move to weekly progression, and when a scheduled deload becomes something you plan instead of something that happens to you.

Before you start

Get the movement patterns coached or filmed. Two sessions with a decent coach — or one honest side-on video per lift, reviewed against a good tutorial — is worth more than any program detail on this page. This article is general information, not medical advice; if something hurts sharply or persistently, see a professional rather than adding 5 kg.
